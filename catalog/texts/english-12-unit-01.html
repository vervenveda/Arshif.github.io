Extended reading · Authority and the missing voice

On the day the town archive opened, a curator described a signed memorandum as the definitive account of a century-old agreement. The document recorded what officials had decided, who had signed, and the terms they expected to enforce. Its precise language gave the appearance of completeness. Yet Laleh noticed that residents most affected by the decision appeared nowhere among its signatories.

She found a later interview in which one resident remembered the agreement as a relief after years of uncertainty. Another recalled that the same terms limited access to shared land. Neither memory could be treated as a transcript of the original meeting. The memorandum, meanwhile, could establish the formal decision without settling how it had been experienced.

In her draft, Laleh had written that the agreement brought peace to everyone. She revised the sentence to say that the agreement formalized a settlement but surviving evidence revealed divergent experiences of its consequences. The revision did not weaken her argument. It changed a claim about every person into a claim defensible from the available record.

Senior seminar and scholarly response

  1. Distinguish institutional authority from evidence of lived experience.
  2. Develop two plausible readings and evaluate the strongest limitations of each.
  3. Write a 250–350-word interpretation proposing what further primary sources could test your claim, without inventing them.
