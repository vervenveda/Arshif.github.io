Six Ways Into the Hall

Open to every reader. Structured for serious study.

The archive itself has no age gate. Formal academic expectations begin with high-school work and rise through advanced undergraduate seminar/research practice.

Readiness note: “All ages” means the archive can be explored by families and independent readers; it does not mean every text is developmentally appropriate for every reader. Some works contain war, enslavement, sexual coercion, religious controversy, oppression, death, or other mature material. Families and educators should select texts according to readiness and context.

Thirty-Three Module Spine

A world-humanities curriculum built from the archive.

The same modules can be entered at different levels. High-school learners emphasize evidence, comparison, context, rhetoric, and analytical writing; university learners add scholarship, bibliography, theory, translation/reception, seminar leadership, and independent research.

Eleven Scholarly Skills

Track the practices that make humanities study rigorous.

Skills remain separate rather than collapsing progress into one score. Choose the active academic pathway, then mark each skill as not observed, developing, or secure.

Research Studio

Eleven humanities tools for reading, writing, and scholarship.

These generators provide structured prompts and templates. They do not fabricate quotations, citations, historical facts, translations, or scholarly sources.

Alignment & Reference Vault

What the formal pathways are designed to support.

High-school alignment uses public standards/frameworks as reference points. University alignment is skills-based because universities determine their own courses, majors, credits, and degree requirements.

This Hall is not an official AP, IB, Cambridge, university, accreditation, or state-credit provider. It can support preparation and curriculum design, but formal program recognition depends on the relevant school, jurisdiction, examination program, or institution.
